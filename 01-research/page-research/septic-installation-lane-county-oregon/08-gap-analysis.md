## Top 5 Highest-Priority Gaps (all scored 20+)

### Gap 1: Dedicated Lane County Install Page [Score 25]
No competitor has a page titled, structured, or optimized for "septic installation Lane County Oregon." D&D's `/septic-installation-lane-county-oregon` is the first dedicated page in this SERP position. This is table stakes — the page must exist and be properly structured. Combined with the Lane County permit fee and process content, this page immediately becomes the most informative contractor resource for this query.

### Gap 2: Lane County Permit Fee Transparency [Score 25]
The Lane County site evaluation fee is **$866** (confirmed effective September 2026, Board Order 26-07-14-17). Zero competitors publish this. Zero cost-guide publishers have a Lane County-specific figure. Stating "the Lane County Subsurface Sanitation Program charges $866 for a septic site evaluation as of September 2026" is a statement no competitor can make with that specificity — and it answers a real question homeowners have before budgeting for an install.

### Gap 3: Plain-Language Two-Step Permit Process [Score 25]
The Lane County two-step process (site evaluation → construction permit → install → inspection) is completely absent from all competitor pages and only sparsely described on the government page itself. A numbered-step guide written for a homeowner who has never done this before is zero-competition content that also qualifies for `HowTo` schema.

### Gap 4 (tied): Real Cost Ranges by System Type [Score 20]
Every competitor hides behind "free estimate." Publishing honest, clearly-labeled general ranges (conventional: $3,000–$10,000; ATT: $12,000–$30,000+) with a clear explanation of what drives the difference is the highest-trust content move available — consistent with every prior page in this pipeline. The $866 permit fee should be explicitly mentioned as a separate, real, government cost the contractor cannot influence.

### Gap 5 (tied): Conventional vs. ATT in Plain Language [Score 20]
Zero of 10 competitors explain what drives the conventional vs. ATT choice in terms a homeowner can understand. Western Oregon context (clay soil, high water table, wet winters, proximity to sensitive water) makes this especially relevant to Lane County buyers. The comparison table from the base septic pack should be carried forward to this page — it is still the highest-value single content block and remains completely unclaimed.

### Gap 6 (tied): DEQ Credential Education + Verification [Score 20]
Every competitor who holds a DEQ credential just asserts it without explaining what it means, why it exists, or how to verify it. The public DEQ license search tool (oregon.gov/deq/residential/pages/onsite-search.aspx) lets anyone confirm a contractor's DEQ status in under a minute — and no competitor links to it. Publishing a one-paragraph explanation of what the DEQ Installer Certification requires (Chemeketa course, exam, 3-year recertification) and linking to the verification tool is a trust move with zero competitive overlap.

### Gap 7 (tied): Rural Lane County Positioning [Score 20]
Lane County's own official language says "many homes and businesses in the County are in rural areas without access to municipal sewer systems." Yet no competitor has content specifically addressing rural buyers. Communities like Veneta, Creswell, Lowell, Oakridge, McKenzie River corridor, and dozens of small unincorporated areas depend entirely on on-site septic — and no contractor page acknowledges their specific situation (larger lots, well setbacks, challenging soils, no city sewer option). SOS Septic covers rural Lane County environmental conditions well but is pumping-only; D&D can own this content space for installation.
