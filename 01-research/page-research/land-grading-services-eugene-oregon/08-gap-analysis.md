## Gap Detail Profiles

### Gap 1: Oregon 811 Utility Locate Compliance
- **Competitor Coverage:** 1/10 — only Eugene Dirt Works mentions utilities in a FAQ question without specifics
- **Conversion Impact:** 7 — trust-builder; answers homeowner anxiety about utility damage
- **Difficulty:** 2 — Oregon811.com is a public resource; straightforward to cite
- **Score:** 31.5
- **Recommended treatment:** Add 2–3 sentences in process section: "Before any grading begins, we submit a utility locate request to Oregon 811 (two business days required by Oregon law) to mark underground lines and protect your property." Add to FAQ: "Do you call 811 before starting?"

### Gap 2: Lane County Permit Specifics (50 cu yd threshold)
- **Competitor Coverage:** 1/10 — no competitor covers this on a grading page
- **Conversion Impact:** 8 — answers a critical pre-call research question; builds authority; reduces hesitation
- **Difficulty:** 3 — Lane County LMD is public record; data is verifiable
- **Score:** 24.0
- **Recommended treatment:** Dedicated subsection or FAQ question: "Do I need a grading permit in Lane County?" with the 50 cu yd rule, Type I/II distinction, LMD contact, and note on processing timeline.

### Gap 3: Malpass Clay Soil Context
- **Competitor Coverage:** 1/10 — zero competitors mention Malpass clay
- **Conversion Impact:** 9 — directly explains the dominant Eugene drainage problem; validates why homeowners have soggy yards; triggers recognition ("that's my yard!")
- **Difficulty:** 3 — data from BLM Technical Note 447 is public; needs condensing for homeowner audience
- **Score:** 27.0
- **Recommended treatment:** 1–2 paragraph section: "Why Eugene Yards Stay Wet" or "Grading in Willamette Valley Clay Soil." Explain Malpass clay properties in plain language. Position D&D as experienced with local conditions.

### Gap 4: Wet Season Grading Timing (Oct–April)
- **Competitor Coverage:** 1/10 — zero competitors address seasonal grading scheduling
- **Conversion Impact:** 7 — answers "when should I schedule grading?" and "can you do it in winter?"
- **Difficulty:** 2 — Oregon OSHA and Portland.gov publish erosion control requirements; May–September optimal window is straightforward
- **Score:** 31.5
- **Recommended treatment:** Short FAQ answer: "When is the best time to grade my property in Oregon?" Explain Oct–Apr wet season, erosion control requirements, and why May–September is ideal for most projects.

### Gap 5: Cost Ranges (per sq ft, per acre, project type)
- **Competitor Coverage:** 1/10 — zero competitors show cost ranges; Eugene Dirt Works only shows equipment rental
- **Conversion Impact:** 9 — cost is the #1 pre-call research question; providing ranges with context converts middle-funnel researchers to callers
- **Difficulty:** 4 — national data available; Eugene-specific data requires framing
- **Score:** 20.3
- **Recommended treatment:** Educational cost section with national ranges clearly labeled as reference points. "Typical projects in Lane County..." with explicit caveat to call for a site-specific free estimate. Include: $0.08–$2.00/sq ft (yard grading), $1,000–$5,125 (driveway), $1,000–$6,700 (foundation prep), $15,800–$44,535/acre (major grading). Note Malpass clay as a cost factor.

### Gap 6: Cut vs Fill Explanation
- **Competitor Coverage:** 1/10 — not covered by any Eugene competitor
- **Conversion Impact:** 6 — educational; answers technical question; builds expertise signal
- **Difficulty:** 3 — standard industry terminology; easy to explain simply
- **Score:** 18.0
- **Recommended treatment:** 2–3 sentences in process section or glossary callout: "Cut and fill balances your site — we excavate high spots (cut) and use that material to build up low areas (fill), minimizing hauled-off material and cost."

### Gap 7: Rough Grade vs Finish Grade Distinction
- **Competitor Coverage:** 1/10 — not covered by any Eugene competitor
- **Conversion Impact:** 7 — answers "what do I need and when?" for new construction clients; positions D&D as knowledgeable
- **Difficulty:** 3 — well-documented trade distinction; needs consumer-friendly language
- **Score:** 23.3
- **Recommended treatment:** Short educational section or FAQ: "What's the difference between rough grade and finish grade?" Rough = bulk earthmoving, sets pad elevation, tolerates ±0.1 ft. Finish = precision pass after utilities, holds ±0.5 in. Both can be done by D&D.

### Gap 8: Building Pad vs Lot Grading Distinction
- **Competitor Coverage:** 2/10 — Eugene Dirt Works mentions building pads briefly; no competitor fully explains the distinction
- **Conversion Impact:** 8 — new construction clients search specifically for "building pad grading"; this term appears in secondary keyword list
- **Difficulty:** 3 — straightforward concept; needs local application
- **Score:** 21.3
- **Recommended treatment:** Callout block or service card: "Building Pad Grading" as a named sub-service. Explain: a pad is the leveled, compacted platform for a home, shop, or outbuilding. Different from general lot grading (drainage correction) — a pad must meet engineered elevation specs.

### Gap 9: Drainage Grading Specifics (swales, French drains)
- **Competitor Coverage:** 3/10 — Purple Heart and Eugene Dirt Works touch on drainage; no one fully explains the methods
- **Conversion Impact:** 9 — drainage is the #2 driver of residential grading calls (after new construction)
- **Difficulty:** 4 — somewhat covered nationally but local soil context needed
- **Score:** 15.8
- **Recommended treatment:** Dedicated drainage grading section explaining how grading alone (slope correction) handles surface water, and when a French drain or drainage swale is needed in addition. Connect to Malpass clay — "In Eugene's clay soil, a slope correction alone may not prevent ponding — we often combine grading with drainage infrastructure."

### Gap 10: CCB License Trust Signal
- **Competitor Coverage:** 4/10 — half the competitors hide their CCB number or don't show it
- **Conversion Impact:** 9 — for homeowners who know what CCB means, it's the primary hire/no-hire signal
- **Difficulty:** 1 — D&D already has CCB #261742; just needs to be displayed prominently
- **Score:** 54.0 (highest priority)
- **Recommended treatment:** CCB #261742 in hero/header area or trust bar. "Licensed, Bonded & Insured — CCB #261742" as a repeating trust element. Link to orccb.org so homeowners can verify. Also include in schema.
