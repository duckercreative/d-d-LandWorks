# Intent Triangulation — D&D Land Works Grading & Leveling (`/grading-leveling/`)

**Sourcing note (same limitation as all three prior packs):** scraped 1-3 star competitor review text and direct Reddit thread content were not retrievable through this session's tools. This file triangulates intent from: (1) homeowner-drainage-problem literature (Magnolia Landscape Construction, Upgraded Home, ITM Landscape, House Digest) that reflects real, aggregated homeowner pain points, (2) cost-guide sources' own framing of buyer decision points (HomeGuide, Angi, HomeAdvisor), (3) Oregon's actual adopted drainage-code standard, and (4) the structural fact — confirmed directly in `04-competitor-matrix.md` — that zero competitors address this buyer at all as a distinct persona from a new-construction excavation buyer.

**This page's buyer is a different mindset than the homepage/site-prep/excavation pages' buyer.** Those pages' buyers are mid-project (new build, new dig, active site). This page's buyer already has a house, already has a yard, and has a problem that's been sitting there — sometimes for years — that they've just decided to finally deal with. That reframes almost every pain point and trigger below relative to the three prior packs.

## Table 3 — Trust Killers & Rebuttals

| Trust killer | Why it matters here | D&D rebuttal (explicit, evidence-based — no invented stats) |
|---|---|---|
| No competitor explains what a "positive drainage slope" actually is | Homeowners are told they need "better drainage" or "more slope" without ever being told what the actual standard is | Cite Oregon's own adopted code standard (ORSC §R401.3) in plain language: a minimum 6-inch fall over the first 10 feet near a foundation, tapering to a 2% minimum slope beyond that — a real, verifiable number, not a vague reassurance |
| Fear this job is too small to matter to a company set up for bigger excavation work | Every competitor's homepage language skews toward foundations, subdivisions, and new construction | State plainly that a yard-sized regrading job is scoped and treated the same as any larger project — reused pattern from the homepage/excavation packs, applied specifically to this page's smaller typical job size |
| Permit anxiety turning a simple fix into a perceived ordeal | No competitor addresses permit thresholds for small jobs at all | State clearly that most yard regrading jobs stay well under Lane County's 50-cubic-yard/2:1-slope grading-permit trigger, while still honestly noting that a large, deep full-property regrade could approach it |
| Confusion about whether to call a landscaper or an excavation contractor | Zero competitors — and no landscaping-company content found either — resolve this for the reader | Directly answer "is this a landscaping job or an excavation job" as its own FAQ entry, framed around job size/equipment/permanence rather than brand preference |
| Worry that a "cheap fix" (adding dirt, raking) is a bandage, not a real solution | Cost-guide sources document this exact failure pattern (topdressing that doesn't hold once depressions exceed about an inch) | Explain the topdressing-vs-full-regrade threshold honestly, including cases where the cheaper option is genuinely the right call — a scope-honesty move consistent with this project's existing trust-building pattern (see homepage pack §12) |
| No visible acknowledgment that yard drainage problems can actually damage the house | Nobody in the competitive set connects "sloped yard" to real foundation/moisture risk | Cite the documented connection (water pooling at a foundation from negative grade contributes to cracking and moisture intrusion) without exaggerating into a scare-tactic framing |
| Zero reviews / zero online presence at launch | Same unavoidable reality as all three prior packs | Do not fabricate testimonials or a review count — same rule as every prior pack |
