## 1. The Positive-Drainage-Slope Standard — VERIFIED DIRECTLY, not assumed

This is the single most load-bearing fact in this entire pack, and the task brief specifically calls for verifying it rather than assuming the commonly-quoted figure is exact. It checks out exactly as commonly cited.

- **What was checked:** Oregon does not write its own independent residential drainage standard — it adopts the International Residential Code (IRC) with state-specific amendments as the **Oregon Residential Specialty Code (ORSC)**. Section **R401.3 ("Drainage")** governs foundation-area grading and drainage statewide.
- **What the code actually says (fetched directly from ICC's published Oregon code text, 2017, 2021, and 2023 editions — identical substantive language across all three):**
  - Lots must be graded to drain surface water away from foundation walls.
  - The grade must fall a **minimum of 6 inches within the first 10 feet** from the foundation — this works out to roughly a **5% slope**.
  - Where a lot line, wall, slope, or other physical barrier prevents that 6-inch/10-foot fall, the code requires an alternate minimum 5% slope **plus** a drain or swale constructed to ensure drainage away from the structure.
  - **Impervious surfaces** (patios, walks, driveways) within 10 feet of the foundation must be sloped **not less than 2%** away from the building.
  - Beyond the immediate 10-foot foundation zone, general professional/industry practice (not itself a distinct numbered code requirement) continues the 2% minimum slope out to the lot line or a drainage facility.
- **Verdict: the commonly-quoted "6 inches over 10 feet" and "2% minimum" figures are both accurate, not folk approximations**, and Oregon has carried this exact language across at least three consecutive code cycles (2017 → 2023), meaning it is stable, not a figure at risk of having just changed.
- **Source:** [ICC — 2023 Oregon Residential Specialty Code, Chapter 4, R401.3 Drainage](https://codes.iccsafe.org/s/ORRC2023P1/chapter-4-foundations/ORRC2023P1-Pt03-Ch04-SecR401.3) · [ICC — 2017 Oregon Residential Specialty Code, R401.3](https://codes.iccsafe.org/s/ORRSC2017P1/chapter-4-foundations/ORRSC2017P1-Pt03-Ch04-SecR401.3) · [ICC — 2024 IRC (model code), R401.3](https://codes.iccsafe.org/s/IRC2024P2/chapter-4-foundations/IRC2024P2-Pt03-Ch04-SecR401.3) · verified 2026-09-08

## 2. Lane County Grading Permit Threshold, Applied Honestly to a Small Yard Job

- **Reused verbatim from the site-preparation pack** (do not re-verify the underlying figure, it was already sourced there): the Lane County Grading and Fill Permit is triggered by moving **more than 50 cubic yards** of earth, or creating a cut/fill slope **steeper than 2:1**.
- **What this means applied specifically to a small yard-regrading job (new analysis for this page):** 50 cubic yards is a meaningful volume — for scale, a 1,000-square-foot yard regraded 6 inches deep (a fairly aggressive full-depth regrade for that size) works out to roughly 18-19 cubic yards, well under the threshold. Even a large, 5,000-square-foot backyard regraded 6 inches deep works out to roughly 90+ cubic yards, which could exceed it. **The honest, non-oversimplified statement for this page is: most ordinary yard-leveling and small-lawn regrading jobs stay well under Lane County's 50-cubic-yard permit threshold, but a large or unusually deep full-property regrade could approach or exceed it — worth confirming for a bigger job, not something to assume either way without checking.** This is a genuine trust/simplicity signal (removing a perceived bureaucratic barrier for the *typical* job this page targets) without overstating it into a blanket "never needs a permit" claim, which would not be accurate for every job size this page could plausibly serve.
- **Source:** [Willamette Craftworks — Lane County Building Permits: 2026 Homeowner's Guide](https://willamettecraftworks.com/permitting/lane-county-or/) (reused from site-preparation pack, third-party-sourced summary of Lane County code — flag for direct Lane County LMD confirmation before publishing as an exact, quotable figure, same caveat as the site-preparation pack) · Lane County Land Management Division · verified (reused) 2026-09-08

## 3. Willamette Valley Clay Soil — Reused, Applied to This Page's Smaller-Scale Context

- **Reused verbatim from the homepage pack** (do not re-derive): Willamette Valley soils are widely documented (OSU Extension) as clay-heavy — high water-holding capacity, poor pore space/drainage, prone to surface ponding, hardens when dry and becomes sticky/heavy when wet.
- **Why this matters specifically for standalone yard grading (new application, not a new fact):** clay soil is a major reason existing yards develop drainage problems in the first place — a yard built on clay with even a slightly-off original grade will pond and stay soggy far more readily than one on well-draining soil, which helps explain why this is such a common, recurring homeowner complaint in this specific region rather than a rare defect.
- **Source:** [OSU Extension Service — Clay Soil Challenges and Solutions for Oregon Gardeners](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/news/clay-soil-challenges-solutions-oregon-gardeners) (reused from homepage pack) · verified (reused) 2026-09-08

## 4. Seasonal Timing — Reused, Scaled Down for a Smaller Job

- **Reused verbatim from the homepage pack:** NOAA's Eugene climate normals show December (7.21 in.), January (6.05 in.), and November (5.98 in.) as the wettest months; May-October is the standard-recommended dry window for excavation, grading, and compaction-dependent work in the Willamette Valley.
- **Applied honestly to this page (new framing, not a new fact):** a small yard-leveling job is far more schedule-flexible than a full excavation or site-prep project — it does not require the same depth of compaction-dependent work, so while the dry-season logic still generally applies (clay grades and compacts better dry), it is not the same hard constraint it is for a foundation dig or a multi-week site-prep project. State this proportionally, not with the same urgency as the excavation/site-prep pages' framing.
- **Source:** [NOAA / National Weather Service — Eugene Climate Data](https://www.weather.gov/media/pqr/climate/ClimateBookEugene/pg61.pdf) (reused from homepage pack) · verified (reused) 2026-09-08

## 5. What was checked and found NOT applicable (do not cite)

- **DEQ 1200-C Construction Stormwater Permit** — the site-preparation pack's 1-acre-disturbance stormwater permit is a construction-project-scale trigger; an ordinary yard regrade of a few thousand square feet does not approach the 1-acre threshold and this permit should not be raised on this page as a live concern (it can be mentioned only as a "not applicable at this scale" contrast if useful, not presented as something the typical reader needs to worry about).
- **Oregon811 / utility locates** — a genuine, load-bearing authority signal for the Excavation & Grading page (new digging near unmarked utilities), but a shallow surface regrade that isn't cutting below existing turf/topsoil generally does not carry the same utility-strike risk profile as a foundation dig or trench. Do not import the excavation page's Oregon811 content wholesale here without confirming with David whether D&D's actual yard-grading depth ever approaches utility-strike risk — if it does on some jobs (deeper cuts near a property's utility laterals), a brief, honest mention may be warranted, but it should not be positioned as this page's primary authority signal the way it is on the Excavation & Grading page.
- **OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P (trench safety)** — applies to trenches and excavations, not surface grading; not relevant to this page's typical scope and should not be cited here just because it appears on the sibling excavation page.
