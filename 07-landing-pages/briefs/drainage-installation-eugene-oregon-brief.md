#### H2 #1: What Is a French Drain, and How Does It Work?

**Traces to:** Gap score 27.0 (per `08-gap-analysis.md`, gap #12 "material specificity"); question mine Q24 ("What's the difference between a French drain and a curtain drain?"); co-occurrence: zero competitors define French drain specifically  
**Format:** Answer-first paragraph + bulleted component list + scope-boundary statement (swale vs. French drain)  
**Speakable:** Yes — on the answer-first paragraph  
**Schema unlock:** FAQPage (Q7 from shortlist)

**Answer-first (40–60 words):**
A French drain is a buried system: a perforated pipe surrounded by washed gravel and wrapped in geotextile filter fabric, installed in a trench with a slight downhill slope. Water enters the gravel and pipe from the surrounding soil, then flows to a discharge point. The whole system depends on three things being right — the slope, the fabric, and where the water exits.

**Bulleted component list (parallel, 5–7 items):**
- Perforated pipe (rigid PVC preferred over corrugated in Willamette Valley clay — PVC holds shape as clay shifts seasonally)
- ¾-inch angular washed drain rock (not pea gravel — the angular shape maintains void space)
- Geotextile filter fabric (wraps the gravel bed, preventing clay silt from migrating in and blocking the pipe over time)
- ~1% downhill slope — approximately 1 inch of drop per 8–10 feet of run
- A real discharge destination (daylight outlet, dry well, or permitted storm tie-in)

**Scope-boundary statement (2 sentences):** A swale is a surface channel that redirects runoff across the ground. A French drain is a buried system that captures and moves water through the soil. If a regrading contractor recommended a French drain after regrading didn't solve the problem, these are two different tools for two different problems.

**Standard installation depth note:** Residential yard French drains typically run 18–24 inches deep. A drain tied to a footing depth follows that footing — commonly 4–6 feet in Lane County. A shallow curtain drain intercepting near-surface water may run as shallow as 8–12 inches.

#### H2 #3: Where Does the Water From a French Drain Go?

**Traces to:** Gap score 50.0 (gap #2 — page's central trust signal; zero competitor covers this); question mine Q29 ("Where does the water go — daylight, dry well, or storm drain?"); forum proxy Q58  
**Format:** Answer-first + bulleted discharge options (3) + Eugene permit callout  
**Speakable:** Yes — highest-priority Speakable target on this page  
**Schema unlock:** FAQPage (Q5 from shortlist) + ItemList (discharge options)

**Answer-first (50–65 words — Speakable, standalone citable):**
> Water from a French drain exits in one of three ways: (1) a daylight discharge point where the pipe exits above ground at a lower elevation and gravity does the rest, (2) a dry well — a buried chamber that slowly infiltrates water back into the soil, or (3) a permitted tie-in to the city's public stormwater system. Option (3) in Eugene requires a permit.

**Bulleted discharge options (ItemList schema, 3 items):**

- **Daylight discharge point** — the pipe runs to a lower point on the property and opens above ground. Requires a real slope to work. A pop-up emitter caps the outlet, opening when water flows and closing otherwise. No permit required for a daylight outlet on private property.
- **Dry well (soakaway)** — a buried gravel-filled chamber that infiltrates water into the surrounding soil. Works well on properties without enough grade for daylight discharge. Generally exempt from Oregon's UIC regulations (per Oregon DEQ). Cost: $1,165–$4,420 nationally depending on size and depth.
- **Permitted storm tie-in** — the pipe connects to the city's public stormwater system at the curb or storm drain. In Eugene, this is regulated under Municipal Code §6.610 and requires a permit issued by the City Engineer. See the permit section below.

**Why the discharge destination matters (2–3 sentences):** A French drain that dead-ends without a real discharge point does not work. Water has to go somewhere — and if the system doesn't have a designed outlet, it concentrates in the gravel bed and eventually saturates the surrounding area, creating a new wet spot. The discharge destination is not an afterthought; it is the design.

#### H2 #6: What Makes Drainage Installation Different in Eugene's Soil?

**Traces to:** Gap score 36.0 (gap #4 "Eugene clay context — Malpass clay + filter fabric"); question mine Q18 ("Does filter fabric matter more in Eugene clay?"), Q19 ("What pipe type is best in Willamette Valley?"); entity: Malpass Clay (VALIDATED_LOCAL)  
**Format:** Answer-first + Malpass clay callout + filter fabric necessity + seasonal timing note  
**Speakable:** Yes — on Malpass clay paragraph  
**Schema unlock:** FAQPage (Q7 from shortlist)

**Answer-first (30–50 words):**
Eugene's soil — specifically the Malpass clay series documented in west Eugene — is the reason drainage installation in Lane County is not the same job as drainage installation anywhere else. Low permeability, high shrink-swell, and seasonal saturation mean two things: the system has to be built right the first time, and filter fabric is not optional.

**Malpass clay paragraph (Speakable candidate):**
Malpass clay — documented by BLM in Technical Note 447 — is the dominant clay series in west Eugene. It has high shrink-swell properties and low permeability. In wet season, the water table in low-lying areas like River Road and Cal Young rises enough that the clay stays saturated for months. Water doesn't drain through it naturally. That's what makes buried drainage systems — not just regrading — the solution for chronically wet yards in this part of Lane County.

**Filter fabric necessity paragraph:**
Without geotextile filter fabric wrapped around the gravel bed, fine clay particles migrate into the drain rock and eventually into the pipe perforations. In sandy soil this takes decades. In Malpass clay, it happens faster. The result is a system that clogs from the inside out and needs to be excavated and rebuilt. A properly built French drain in Eugene uses filter fabric — not as an upgrade, but as a requirement.

**Seasonal installation note:** The optimal installation window in Willamette Valley clay is May through October when the ground is dry enough to hold a trench wall. D&D excavates year-round; wet-season work is possible but adds dewatering considerations and may extend the job schedule. If your timeline is flexible, a May–September start is the easiest ground to work in.

#### H2 #7: What Materials Does a Properly Built Drain Use?

**Traces to:** Gap score 27.0 (gap #12 "material specificity — pipe, gravel, slope"); question mine Q19 ("What pipe type is best in Willamette Valley?"); Matrix A: 0/13 competitors show material specificity  
**Format:** Answer-first + bulleted materials list + slope requirement  
**Speakable:** No (secondary section — informational, not featured-snippet-format)  
**Schema unlock:** ItemList (materials list)

**Answer-first (30–40 words):**
A French drain that lasts in Eugene's clay uses four things right: the correct pipe, clean angular gravel, geotextile filter fabric on all sides, and enough slope to drain. Each one matters more in Willamette Valley clay than in easier-draining soil.

**Bulleted materials list (ItemList schema):**
- **Rigid perforated PVC pipe** — 4-inch diameter for residential; installed with holes facing down. Rigid PVC is preferred over corrugated in Malpass clay because clay's seasonal shrink-swell can crush corrugated pipe over time.
- **¾-inch angular washed drain rock** (also called drain gravel or clean crushed rock) — the angular shape maintains void space so water moves through. Pea gravel rounds off and compacts.
- **Geotextile filter fabric** — wraps the entire gravel bed and pipe assembly. Prevents clay silt from migrating into the system. In Willamette Valley clay, this is the part contractors skip on cheap jobs — and it's why those jobs fail.
- **~1% slope** — approximately 1 inch of drop per 8–10 feet of run. Less than 1% and water stagnates; more than 1% and erosion can develop at the outlet.
- **A designed discharge point** — daylight outlet, dry well, or permitted storm tie-in. The system does not work without a real destination for the water.

#### H2 #8: Is This the Same Drain My Foundation Requires?

**Traces to:** Gap score 27.0 (gap #8 "French drain vs. foundation drain ORSC R405 disambiguation"); question mine Q30 ("Is a French drain the same as the drain required around my foundation?"); anti-doorway gate — Eugene-specific FAQ

**Speakable:** Yes — on the answer-first paragraph  
**Schema unlock:** FAQPage (Q8 from shortlist)

**Answer-first (40–60 words):**
Not exactly. Oregon's residential building code (ORSC R405) requires a perimeter drain around new foundation walls — a system physically similar to a French drain but required by the building permit, not chosen as a standalone yard drainage fix. A yard French drain you're installing to solve a drainage problem is a different scope and does not require a building permit.

**One-paragraph explanation:** The ORSC R405 foundation drain is triggered by a new foundation build. It runs around the footing perimeter and connects to a discharge point to protect the foundation from hydrostatic pressure. If you're building new and need both the foundation excavation and the foundation drain as part of a code-required scope, see D&D's [foundation excavation Eugene Oregon page](/foundation-excavation-eugene-oregon/). If you're dealing with a wet yard on an existing home, that's what this page covers.
