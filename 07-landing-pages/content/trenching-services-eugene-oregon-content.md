## How Deep Do Irrigation Lines Need to Be in Lane County, Oregon?

Irrigation lines in Lane County, Oregon are typically buried 6 to 8 inches deep for mainlines and 4 to 6 inches for laterals. Oregon has no state code minimum depth for irrigation — unlike water service lines, which must be 24 inches deep per OPSC 609.1. Oregon law (ORS 757.545) requires anyone excavating — including irrigation trenching on private property — to notify Oregon 811 at least two business days before digging.

Here's what drives those depths in Willamette Valley clay:

**Frost is not the controlling factor for irrigation lines.** Lane County's frost depth is approximately 12 inches. But irrigation systems in this valley are winterized by draining or blowing out — not buried below frost depth. The homeowners who ask "do I need to go 24 inches deep for my sprinklers?" are thinking of water service lines, which are a different code category entirely. Irrigation pipe at 6–8 inches survives a Lane County winter fine as long as you drain the system before a hard freeze.

**Root intrusion is the controlling factor.** Malpass silty clay loam — the dominant soil in the Willamette Valley floor — retains moisture and supports deep root systems from established trees, shrubs, and turf. Mainlines at 6–8 inches stay below most active root zones. Where the trench runs under mature trees, we go deeper.

**Equipment matters for established lawns.** A vibratory plow installs pipe at 6–8 inches with a slit cut that closes back over itself — minimal turf disturbance. A chain trencher leaves a strip that needs to be restored. We use the method that fits the conditions, not the one that's fastest for us.

| Line type | Typical burial depth (Lane County) |
|---|---|
| Irrigation mainline | 6–8 inches |
| Irrigation lateral | 4–6 inches |
| Drip / micro tubing | Surface to 4 inches |
| Water service line | 24 inches (OPSC 609.1) — separate code requirement |

## Oregon 811 — Required Before Any Trenching, Including Irrigation

Oregon law (ORS 757.545) requires notification before any excavation. The legal definition of "excavation" under ORS 757.541 covers any operation in which earth is moved or displaced — which includes an irrigation trench on your own residential property.

Most homeowners assume 811 is only for contractors running utility lines. It isn't. A 6-inch irrigation trench can still cross a buried cable, gas line, or electrical conduit that the homeowner has forgotten about or never knew was there. Lane County has decades of buried infrastructure that doesn't always show up on a property survey.

**How it works:** Call 811 or submit a locate request at [oregon811.org](https://oregon811.org). Do it at least 2 business days before you want to dig. The ticket is valid for 30 calendar days. Utility companies locate and mark their lines within that window. D&D calls 811 before every project as a standard step — it's not optional, and it's not a delay, it's just what you do before the machine goes in the ground.

Calling 811 is free. A utility strike is not.

## Ditch Digging in Eugene and Lane County

"Ditch digging" is the right term for drainage channels, perimeter ditches, and agricultural water conveyance — open, wider profiles that move water rather than route a pipe. D&D handles these for residential and rural properties throughout Lane County.

Common scenarios:

- **Drainage ditch after a wet winter.** Property sits low, water pools near the foundation or along the driveway edge. A properly graded outfall ditch moves runoff to daylight.
- **Rural property perimeter drainage.** Acreage east of Eugene often needs a perimeter ditch on the low side of a sloped lot to intercept uphill flow.
- **Agricultural irrigation ditch.** Gravity-fed water conveyance for larger lots — less common but D&D handles the excavation.

If the project involves a perforated drain pipe and gravel system (French drain), see our [drainage installation](/drainage-installation-eugene-oregon) page — that covers the full drainage system, not just the open ditch.

**No permit is required** for a drainage ditch on private Lane County property that doesn't cross a county road right-of-way or move more than 50 cubic yards of material. If your ditch borders a county road, a Lane County Facility Permit is required. We'll flag that at the estimate stage.

**Timing note:** drainage ditch requests pile up in October and November when the rains start. If you saw standing water last winter and want to fix it before this wet season, call now — D&D schedules these projects on a first-come basis.

## Do I Need a Permit to Trench in Lane County?

This is the question everyone has before they call, so here's the straight answer:

| Scenario | Permit required? |
|---|---|
| Irrigation trench on private property (not crossing ROW) | **No** |
| Drainage ditch on private property (under 50 CY, not crossing ROW) | **No** |
| Any trench crossing a county road right-of-way | **Yes** — Lane County Facility Permit |
| Work moving more than 50 cubic yards of material | **Possibly** — grading review |

The permit authority for ROW work is the Lane County Land Management Division ([lanecounty.org/departments/pw/land_management](https://lanecounty.org/departments/pw/land_management)).

For most residential irrigation and drainage projects on private Lane County property, no permit is required. If your project falls into a permit situation, we'll tell you at the estimate stage — not after we've already started.

## Best Time to Schedule Trenching in Eugene

Lane County's Malpass silty clay loam is a different animal depending on the season.

**May through September (dry season):** Clay cuts cleanly, trench walls hold, backfill compacts predictably. This is the window most clients target, and it's the right call. Irrigation projects especially benefit from dry-season scheduling — the trench stays open cleanly while the pipe is laid.

**October through April (wet season):** Trenching is possible, but saturated clay makes open trench walls less stable and correct compaction takes more attention. Drainage ditch work often comes in during this window — clients see standing water after the first big rains and call. D&D takes these jobs and works them as conditions allow.

**Scheduling reality:** Spring books up. If you have an irrigation job for summer, call in April. Drainage projects after a wet winter often have a 2–4 week lead time by November. The earlier you're in the queue, the more flexibility we have on scheduling.

## FAQ — Trenching Services in Eugene, Oregon

**How deep do irrigation lines need to be buried in Lane County, Oregon?**
Irrigation mainlines in Lane County are typically buried 6 to 8 inches deep; lateral lines 4 to 6 inches. Oregon has no state code minimum for irrigation line burial depth — unlike water service lines, which must be 24 inches deep per OPSC Section 609.1. The practical depth driver in Willamette Valley clay is root intrusion from established landscaping, not frost.

**What's the difference between a sprinkler mainline trench and a lateral trench?**
The mainline is the primary supply line running from the backflow preventer or zone valve — larger diameter, higher pressure. Laterals are the branch lines running to individual sprinkler heads. Mainlines are buried deeper (6–8 inches) to protect them; laterals can be shallower (4–6 inches) since they carry less pressure and are typically in garden beds or turf.

**How much does it cost to trench 100 feet for irrigation?**
A 100-foot irrigation trench in Lane County typically falls in the $1,200–$2,500 range — trench, backfill, and compaction, not including pipe, fittings, or irrigation components. Variables: soil conditions, whether the trench crosses hardscape, and site access.

**Do I need to call Oregon 811 before digging an irrigation trench?**
Yes. Oregon law (ORS 757.545) requires anyone excavating — including irrigation trenching on private property — to notify Oregon 811 at least 2 business days before digging. Call 811 or submit at [oregon811.org](https://oregon811.org). It's free. D&D calls 811 before every project as a standard step.

**Do I need a permit to dig an irrigation trench in Lane County?**
For most residential irrigation trenches on private property that don't cross a county road right-of-way, no permit is required in Lane County. If the trench crosses a county road, a Lane County Facility Permit is needed through the Lane County Land Management Division. Projects moving more than 50 cubic yards may trigger a grading review. We'll flag permit requirements at the estimate stage.

**Is trenching the same as ditch digging?**
Often used interchangeably, but trenching typically refers to a narrow, deep cut for utilities or pipe, while ditch digging usually means a wider, open channel for drainage or water conveyance. D&D handles both — narrow irrigation and utility trenches as well as wider drainage ditches for rural and residential Lane County properties.

**Can you trench under a driveway, or do you need to bore?**
D&D can open-cut a driveway trench and restore the surface. For most homeowners crossing a paved driveway, directional boring (trenchless) is less disruptive — no concrete or asphalt to break out and repour. For short crossings under an unpaved driveway, open-cut is usually faster and lower cost. We can discuss which method fits your project at the estimate.

**Can I DIY my irrigation trench, or should I hire a contractor?**
For short, shallow runs under 50 feet with no utilities nearby and dry conditions, DIY with a rented walk-behind trencher is reasonable. For longer runs, wet Lane County clay, near-utility work, or hardscape crossings, a professional saves time and avoids compaction problems. Either way: call Oregon 811 before any digging.
