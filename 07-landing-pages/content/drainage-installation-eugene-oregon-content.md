## What Is a French Drain, and How Does It Work?

A French drain is a buried system: a perforated pipe surrounded by washed gravel and wrapped in geotextile filter fabric, installed in a trench with a slight downhill slope. Water enters the gravel and pipe from the surrounding soil, then flows to a discharge point. Three things have to be right for the system to work: the slope, the filter fabric, and where the water exits.

**What goes into a properly built French drain:**

- **Rigid perforated PVC pipe**, 4-inch diameter for most residential work, installed holes-down. Rigid PVC is preferred over corrugated in Willamette Valley clay because Malpass clay's seasonal shrink-swell can crush corrugated pipe over time.
- **Three-quarter-inch angular washed drain rock** around the pipe. The angular shape maintains void space so water can move through. Round pea gravel compacts and closes off.
- **Geotextile filter fabric** wrapped around the entire gravel bed. In Eugene's clay soil, this is the part that determines whether the system lasts 15 to 25 years or needs to be excavated and rebuilt in a few.
- **A slope of roughly 1 percent,** about 1 inch of drop per 8 to 10 feet of run.
- **A real discharge destination:** a daylight outlet, a dry well, or a permitted tie-in to the city storm system.

Residential yard French drains typically run 18 to 24 inches deep. A curtain drain intercepting near-surface water can be as shallow as 8 to 12 inches. A French drain tied to a foundation footing follows that footing: commonly 4 to 6 feet in Lane County.

A surface swale redirects runoff across the ground. A French drain moves water through the soil. If a regrading contractor recommended a French drain after regrading didn't solve the problem, those are two different tools for two different problems.

## Where Does the Water From a French Drain Go? {.where-water-goes-answer}

Water from a French drain exits in one of three ways: a daylight discharge point where the pipe exits above ground at a lower elevation and gravity takes over, a dry well that slowly infiltrates water back into the soil, or a permitted tie-in to the city's public stormwater system. The third option in Eugene requires a permit. This question determines the entire system design, and it is the question most homeowners don't think to ask until a contractor asks it back.

A French drain that dead-ends without a real discharge point does not work. Water has to go somewhere. If the system doesn't have a designed outlet, it concentrates in the gravel bed and eventually saturates the surrounding area, creating a new wet spot instead of solving the existing one.

**Three discharge options:**

1. **Daylight discharge point.** The pipe runs to a lower point on the property and opens above ground. Requires a real slope from the wet area to the outlet. A pop-up emitter caps the end, opening when water flows and closing otherwise. No permit required for a daylight outlet on private property.

2. **Dry well (soakaway).** A buried gravel-filled chamber that infiltrates water into the surrounding soil below the drain. Works on properties without enough grade for daylight discharge. French drains, swales, and footing drains are explicitly exempt from Oregon's Underground Injection Control regulations, per Oregon DEQ. No DEQ registration is required for a standard residential French drain discharging to a dry well. National installed cost: $1,165 to $4,420 depending on size and depth.

3. **Permitted storm tie-in.** The pipe connects to the city's public stormwater system at the curb or a storm drain. In Eugene, this is regulated under Municipal Code Section 6.610 and requires a permit from the City Engineer. D&D will tell you if your discharge plan requires this permit and what your options are.

## What Makes Drainage Installation Different in Eugene's Soil? {.malpass-clay-paragraph}

Malpass clay, documented in BLM Technical Note 447, is the dominant soil series in west Eugene. It has high shrink-swell properties and low permeability. In wet season, the water table in low-lying areas like River Road, Cal Young, and Bethel rises enough that the clay stays saturated for months. Water doesn't drain through it naturally. That is what makes buried drainage systems, rather than just regrading, the solution for chronically wet yards in west Eugene.

South Eugene homeowners in Fairmount and the South Hills deal with a different but related problem: hillside runoff pushing against uphill foundation walls. The fix is still a buried system, but the system design is different. The site conditions determine which approach applies.

**The filter fabric issue in Eugene clay:**

Without geotextile filter fabric wrapped around the gravel bed, fine clay particles migrate into the drain rock and eventually into the pipe perforations. In sandy soil this takes decades. In Malpass clay, it happens faster. The result is a system that clogs from the inside out and needs to be excavated and rebuilt rather than cleaned. A properly built French drain in Eugene uses filter fabric, not as an upgrade but as a requirement.

The most common French drain failure in Lane County is not the pipe. It is the absence of filter fabric. The pipe lasts. The gravel lasts. What fails is the infiltration process when fine clay particles seal the void space. A drain installed without filter fabric in Malpass clay is a system that will eventually need to be dug up. The only question is how soon.

**Seasonal installation timing:**

Eugene receives approximately 46.6 inches of rain per year, nearly 9 inches more than the U.S. average, almost all of it falling between October and May. The best installation window in Willamette Valley clay is May through October, when the ground is dry enough to hold a trench wall. D&D installs year-round. Wet-season work is possible but adds dewatering requirements and can extend the schedule. If your timing is flexible, a May through September start is the easiest ground to work in.

## What Materials Does a Properly Built Drain Use?

A French drain that lasts in Eugene's clay uses four things correctly: the right pipe, clean angular gravel, geotextile filter fabric on all sides, and enough slope to drain. Each matters more in Willamette Valley clay than in easier-draining soil.

**Materials list:**

1. **Rigid perforated PVC pipe**, 4-inch diameter for residential, holes facing down. PVC holds shape as clay shifts seasonally. Corrugated pipe costs less but is more vulnerable to clay pressure over time.
2. **Three-quarter-inch angular washed drain rock** around and over the pipe. Angular shape maintains void space for water movement. Pea gravel compacts and closes off.
3. **Geotextile filter fabric** wrapping the full gravel bed and pipe assembly. Prevents clay silt migration into the system. This is the component most often skipped on cheap installs, and the reason those installs fail in Eugene soil.
4. **Roughly 1 percent slope:** approximately 1 inch of drop per 8 to 10 feet of run. Less than that and water stagnates. More and erosion can develop at the outlet.
5. **A designed discharge point:** daylight outlet, dry well, or permitted storm tie-in. The system does not work without a real destination for the water.

## Is This the Same Drain My Foundation Requires?

Not exactly. Oregon's residential building code (ORSC R405) requires a perimeter drain around new foundation walls, triggered by the building permit process. A yard French drain installed to fix a drainage problem on an existing home is a separate scope and does not require a building permit.

The ORSC R405 foundation drain runs around the footing perimeter to protect the foundation from hydrostatic pressure. If you are building new and need both the foundation excavation and the code-required foundation drain, see D&D's [foundation excavation Eugene Oregon page](/foundation-excavation-eugene-oregon/). If you are dealing with a wet yard or foundation edge on an existing home, that is what this page covers.

A sump pump is a separate category: plumbing and electrical trade scope, not excavation. If your situation involves a sump pit, that is a conversation to have with a licensed plumber.
