## What Eugene properties need in a grading contractor

Grading here isn't the same as grading somewhere with sandy soil and eight inches of rain a year. Eugene sits on Malpass clay. It drains slowly, swells when wet, shrinks when dry, and it's all through the lower parts of town. A flat yard on Malpass clay doesn't shed water. It holds it. The Willamette Valley gets 40 to 60 inches of rain a year, mostly between October and April, and if the grade is off, you'll find out come November: water toward the foundation, crawlspace moisture showing up a year after the work was done, mud from October through May.

The regulatory side matters too. Lane County requires a grading permit when a project moves more than 50 cubic yards of earth or creates a cut or fill slope steeper than 2:1. Oregon law requires notifying Oregon 811 at least two business days before any excavation starts. Skipping a required permit can mean fines; starting work without the utility locate is dangerous and expensive when something gets hit. We do both as standard on every job. Before we leave, we verify slope direction and drainage fall at every point on the site.

## Grading services we provide in Lane County

### Lot leveling and yard grading

If water pools in your yard after rain (especially near the foundation or crawlspace), the grade is likely the problem. We regrade residential lots to move surface water away from structures and toward proper outlets. In yards with Malpass clay, common in lower-elevation and west Eugene areas, slope correction alone sometimes isn't enough. We'll tell you upfront if your site needs a French drain or swale alongside the regrading work.

### Building pad grading

A building pad is the leveled, compacted platform your structure sits on, and it has to be right from the start. This isn't yard leveling. A pad must meet specific elevation specs from your architect or engineer, with enough bearing capacity for the structure. We grade building pads for homes, shops, barns, ADUs, and commercial structures throughout Lane County. We work from your engineered drawings and confirm the finished elevation before we leave the site.

### Drainage grading and slope correction

Drainage grading is the most common call we get in Eugene: a yard that holds water after every rain, or water running the wrong direction on a lot. We assess the existing grade, figure out where water goes now versus where it should, and cut new drainage falls to redirect surface runoff. On clay soil sites where slope correction alone won't get complete resolution, we combine regrading with a French drain or swale and explain the options before anything starts.

### Gravel driveway grading and repair

Lane County gravel driveways take a beating every wet season. Potholes form, edges wash out, the crown disappears, water starts running down the middle. We regrade the surface to restore proper drainage, rebuild eroded edges, and redistribute or add gravel as needed. We also grade RV pads, shed pads, and access roads on rural and agricultural properties throughout the county.

### Site grading for new construction

New construction starts with rough grading: stripping topsoil, setting the building pad elevation, cutting and filling to balance material on-site, roughing in drainage falls. After underground utilities are installed, we return for finish grading, the precision pass that brings the surface to within ±0.5 inch and prepares the site for concrete, planting, or final inspection. We work with builders, developers, and owner-builders. For related excavation work, see our [excavation contractor in Eugene](/excavation-contractor-eugene-oregon) page.

## Why Eugene yards stay wet (and how grading fixes it)

Most yards that pool water in Eugene have the same root cause: Malpass clay.

Malpass clay comes from Mt. Mazama, the same eruption that formed Crater Lake. It's dominant in low-lying Eugene, especially west of downtown, and it has two properties that cause ongoing problems for homeowners. First, it drains very slowly. Water sits on top rather than soaking through, especially when the soil is already saturated from weeks of October rain. Second, it has high shrink/swell behavior: it expands when wet and contracts when dry, which shifts grades over time and makes drainage problems worse a couple years after a house goes in.

Regrading is the fix on most sites. Correcting the grade to a 2% slope away from the structure moves surface water before it can pool. On sites with thick, nearly impermeable Malpass clay, combining slope correction with a French drain or drainage swale gets complete resolution; the drain moves water through the clay layer instead of waiting for it to drain on its own.

> Eugene and the Willamette Valley get 40 to 60+ inches of rain a year, most of it between October and April, on soil that doesn't drain quickly. That's why grading matters more here than in most of Oregon.

If you're dealing with a wet yard or water near your foundation, call [541-401-8726](tel:5414018726). A free site visit is the best way to figure out whether slope correction alone will fix it or whether you need additional drainage work.

## Lane County grading permits: what you need to know

Not every grading project needs a permit. But many do, and homeowners find out too late, after work is done and the county sends a notice.

Lane County Land Management Division (LMD) requires a grading permit when either of these applies:

- Your project moves more than 50 cubic yards of earth
- The project creates a cut or fill slope steeper than 2:1

Foundation excavation under an active building permit is generally exempt. Driveway regrading and landscaping grading are not automatically exempt; if you're moving more than 50 cubic yards, a permit applies. LMD is at 3050 N. Delta Hwy, Eugene, OR 97408, open Monday through Friday 9am to 3pm. If your project has a deadline, plan ahead on permit timelines.

**Oregon 811 before any grading starts**

Oregon law requires contractors to notify Oregon 811 (Oregon Utility Notification Center) at least two business days before any grading or excavation begins. Utility companies come out and mark underground lines. No grading starts until the marks are in the ground. We submit the Oregon 811 locate request as a first step on every job, not something that gets skipped when the schedule is tight.

We handle permit guidance for Lane County projects. Call before you start and we'll tell you whether your project crosses the threshold and what the process looks like.

## Our grading process: from site visit to final grade

**1. Site visit and scope**
We walk the property, check slope and drainage direction, look at soil conditions, and confirm whether the project needs a Lane County permit. You get a written scope and estimate before anything starts.

**2. Oregon 811 utility locate**
We submit the locate request at least two business days before equipment arrives. Utility companies mark underground lines. We check that all marks are in before we start. This is Oregon law and we don't skip it.

**3. Rough grading**
Bulk earthmoving: topsoil stripping for building pad projects, setting elevations, cutting high spots, filling low areas. We balance cut and fill material on-site when volume allows. Rough grade tolerance: ±0.1 ft of plan.

**4. Drainage work (when needed)**
French drain, drainage swale, or Oregon wet season erosion control (required Oct 1–Apr 30) happens after rough grade. Silt fence and erosion fabric for any winter work.

**5. Finish grading**
Final pass to ±0.5 inch surface accuracy. Slope direction verified at every point. Water confirmed draining away from structures. Site cleaned up before we leave.

**What is the difference between rough grading and finish grading?**

Rough grading is the bulk earthmoving stage: we set the building pad elevation, strip topsoil, and rough in drainage falls, typically within ±0.1 foot of plan. Finish grading follows after underground utilities are installed. It brings the surface to ±0.5 inch precision and prepares the site for paving, planting, or final inspection. D&D handles both stages for new construction projects in Lane County.

**Do you call Oregon 811 before starting grading work?**

Yes, always. Oregon law requires contractors to notify Oregon 811 (the Utility Notification Center) at least two business days before any excavation or grading begins. We submit the locate request before equipment arrives on your property. Utility companies mark underground lines, and we check that all marks are present before we start. Required by Oregon OSHA; standard on every D&D job.

**Can grading fix standing water in my Eugene yard?**

Usually yes. Water pooling in Willamette Valley yards is most often caused by flat or reverse-sloped grade, especially where Malpass clay slows drainage. We regrade to move water away from your home and toward proper outlets. On sites where slope correction alone won't drain fast enough through heavy clay, we recommend combining regrading with a French drain or swale. **When is the best time to schedule grading in Oregon?**

May through September. Dry conditions reduce erosion risk and keep project costs down. Grading can be done October through April, but Oregon's wet season (Oct 1–Apr 30) requires additional erosion control under state law, which adds time and cost. How saturated the soil is also matters; Malpass clay handles equipment longer than some soils but not when it's fully waterlogged. Spring and summer slots book up early, so contact us ahead of time.
