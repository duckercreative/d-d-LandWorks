## How Deep Does Each Utility Need to Be Buried in Lane County?

In Lane County, burial depth depends on the utility type. Water lines go deepest — 24 inches — driven by the county's 12-inch frost depth. Sewer, gas, and electrical conduit each have their own minimums per Oregon code. {.speakable}

**Lane County Burial Depth Requirements**

| Utility | Minimum Burial Depth | Code Citation | Key Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water service line | **24 inches** below grade | OPSC 609.1 | 12" below Lane County's 12" frost depth; may need deeper under driveways |
| Sanitary sewer lateral | **12 inches** of cover above pipe | OPSC 718.1 | Must slope at least ¼" per foot toward main or septic tank |
| Gas piping — standard | **18 inches** below grade | ORSC G2415.12 | Standard residential gas service runs |
| Gas piping — outdoor appliances | **8 inches** below grade | ORSC G2415.12.1 | Generator feeds, outdoor BBQ/heater drops |
| Electrical conduit (PVC) | **18"** open ground · **24"** under driveways | NEC Table 300.5 | Most residential runs use PVC or nonmetallic raceway |
| Electrical conduit (rigid metal) | **6 inches** | NEC Table 300.5 | Applies in open ground and under driveways |
| Direct-burial cable (UF/USE) | **24 inches** | NEC Table 300.5 | Conduit generally preferred for repullability |
| Communications/fiber conduit | ISP/utility-specified | No Oregon state minimum | Contact your ISP for their conduit spec before digging |

*Source: Lane County Land Management Division — Building Safety Division "Trenching for Utilities" guidance, verified September 2026. Oregon's 2023 Electrical Specialty Code adopts NEC Table 300.5 without amending the numeric depth values.*

## Can All Your Utilities Share One Trench?

Yes — in Lane County, water, sewer, electrical conduit, and gas lines can share a single trench using modern approved pipe materials. Lane County code doesn't prohibit multiple utilities in one trench; it sets one key separation rule: where a water line crosses above a sewer line in the same trench, the bottom of the water pipe must be at least 12 inches above the top of the sewer pipe (OPSC 720.1). For most new residential construction and ADU projects, joint trenching is the standard approach — one mobilization, one open cut, lower total cost. {.speakable}

The separation rule applies at the crossing. When running parallel in the same trench, the water line should maintain a horizontal separation from the sewer line. With modern Schedule 40 PVC or PEX water lines and PVC/ABS sewer pipe, joint trenching is code-compliant and commonly done throughout Lane County.

One honest caveat: individual utility companies — EWEB, NW Natural, PGE — may have their own separation specifications that go beyond code minimums. Confirm those requirements when you initiate your service application with the utility. When you get your estimate from D&D, we can discuss which utilities make sense to run together for your specific project layout.

## Who Calls 811 — and Who Calls the Utility Company?

These are two different calls for two different purposes. Confusing them can hold up a project.

**Call 1: Oregon 811 (Oregon Utility Notification Center)**

Oregon 811 is the free, state-mandated locate service. Utility companies mark the location of underground lines with paint and flags before you dig, so no one cuts a gas main or knocks out power to a neighborhood.

- Under ORS Chapter 757, both the property owner and the excavation contractor are legally required to notify Oregon 811 at least two business days before any digging begins
- The service is free — call **811** (toll-free: 1-800-332-2344) or submit online at digsafelyoregon.com, available 24/7
- Tickets are currently valid for **30 days** — this was reduced from 45 days under an amendment to OAR 952-001-0010(23) in 2025/2026. For projects that run longer than 30 days, Oregon 811 must be re-notified before the ticket expires
- Oregon 811 locates the public utility lines to the meter. It does **not** locate private lines on your property — irrigation systems, outbuilding power, shed electrical. Those are the property owner's responsibility to locate before any excavation starts

D&D calls Oregon 811 on every project, without exception, and manages the 30-day ticket renewal for projects that run longer.

**Call 2: The Utility Company (EWEB, Springfield Utility Board, NW Natural)**

This is a service application to initiate a new connection at the main or the meter — a separate process entirely from the locate call.

- For Eugene water and electric: **EWEB** at (541) 685-7000 | eweb.org
- For Springfield water and electric: **Springfield Utility Board** at (541) 746-8451 | subutil.com
- For gas: **NW Natural** at 800-927-6123 | nwnatural.com

The property owner typically initiates the service application with the utility company. For gas, NW Natural handles the connection from the street main to the meter at the property line — the contractor handles the trench on private property from the meter to the structure. A licensed gas fitter handles the connection, permit, and pressure test. For EWEB water and electric, call early in the project so the utility can schedule any meter placement or tap work on their end before your trench is dug.

D&D handles the trench. The utility company handles the connection at the main or meter. The licensed gas fitter or electrician handles the final connection at your structure.

## What Permits Does Utility Trenching Require in Lane County?

Whether you need a permit depends on where the trench runs, not just what utility it's for. Three different triggers apply in Lane County.

**Scenario 1 — Trench in the public right-of-way (ROW):**

A Lane County **Facility Permit** is required from Lane County Public Works before work begins. This covers any pipe, conduit, or vault installed in a county road right-of-way.

- Processing time: **6–8 weeks** — factor this into your project schedule, especially for ADU projects on a contractor's timeline
- Contact: 3050 N. Delta Hwy, Eugene, OR 97408 | (541) 682-6902
- The permit fee equals actual processing cost, determined by staff and paid before the permit is issued

**Scenario 2 — Trench entirely on private property, tied to a permitted structure:**

Utility trenching associated with a building permit (new construction, ADU, addition) is typically covered under the associated plumbing, mechanical, or electrical permit. No separate trenching permit is required.

**Scenario 3 — Grading scope trigger:**

If the utility trench work involves moving more than 50 cubic yards of earth or creating a cut or fill slope steeper than 2:1, a separate grading permit may be required. Most standard residential utility trenches are well under this threshold.

D&D will help you identify which scenario applies to your project. If a right-of-way permit is required, plan for the 6–8 week processing window before scheduling the trench.

## Eugene Soil and Wet-Season Timing for Utility Trenching

Lane County's Willamette Valley floor is dominated by Malpass silty clay loam — a dense, moisture-retaining soil that performs differently depending on the season. In dry conditions (May through September), it cuts cleanly and compacts well. During Oregon's wet season (October through April), Malpass clay becomes saturated and significantly harder to work with. Open trenches in wet-season clay can fill with water overnight, and compacting saturated backfill properly is difficult without dewatering. This is a real planning consideration, not a contractor excuse.

**When to schedule utility trenching in Lane County:**

The dry-season window — May through September — is the right time to schedule elective utility work in Eugene. Wet-season trenching is feasible for urgent repairs (a broken water service line doesn't wait for May), but dry-season jobs consistently produce better compaction, cleaner surface restoration, and faster scheduling. The backlog that builds in May and June is real — if you are planning an ADU utility connection or a shop electrical run, calling in spring sets you up for a clean dry-season project window.

For emergency utility failures during the wet season, D&D is available — call 541-401-8726.

## What Happens to the Soil After the Trench Is Dug?

Digging a utility trench pulls material out of the ground. What happens with it next depends on the trench depth, the project timeline, and whether the excavated soil can go back in.

**Moving the soil** — Excavated material gets stockpiled beside the open trench while the utility work is underway. On tighter job sites, D&D will stage the spoil to keep it clear of the work area and the property's landscaping. Some excavated material may be hauled off the site if it is unsuitable for reuse or if there is no room to stage it on the property.

**Backfilling the trench** — Once the utility line, pipe, or conduit is in place and the inspection requirement (where applicable) is satisfied, the trench is backfilled. Suitable material is placed back in layers and compacted so the ground above the line settles properly and doesn't sink later. In Lane County's Malpass silty clay loam, proper lift thickness and compaction technique matters — saturated clay that gets dumped back in without compaction will settle unevenly over time, leaving a trench-shaped depression across your lawn or driveway. Bedding sand is used immediately around pipe in most utility trench installations before native soil is replaced above it.

**Finishing the work area** — After the trench is backfilled and compacted, the disturbed surface is rough-graded to match the surrounding grade. If the trench crossed turf or landscaping, that area is left ready for the next stage — seeding, sod, or paving — depending on what the property needs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How deep does a water service line need to be buried in Lane County, Oregon?

Water service lines must be buried at least 24 inches below grade in Lane County — 12 inches below the local 12-inch frost depth, as required by Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code 609.1. Deeper burial may be required under driveways, parking areas, or where the inspector specifies additional depth.

---

### How deep does a sewer lateral need to be buried?

Sanitary sewer laterals need at least 12 inches of cover above the pipe in Lane County (OPSC 718.1). The pipe must also slope at least ¼ inch per foot toward the sewer main or septic tank to ensure gravity drainage. Inadequate slope is the most common cause of sewer lateral failure.

---

### How deep does electrical conduit need to be buried in Oregon?

In Oregon, PVC electrical conduit must be at least 18 inches deep in open ground and 24 inches deep under driveways or parking areas, per NEC Table 300.5 (adopted by Oregon's Electrical Specialty Code). Rigid metal conduit can be as shallow as 6 inches in both conditions. Direct-burial cable requires 24 inches.

---

### How deep does a gas line need to be buried in Oregon?

Standard residential gas piping must be at least 18 inches below grade in Oregon (Oregon Residential Specialty Code G2415.12). Lines feeding portable outdoor appliances — generators, outdoor heaters, BBQ drops — can be as shallow as 8 inches under ORSC G2415.12.1.

---

### Can water, sewer, electrical, and gas go in the same trench?

Yes — joint trenching is code-permitted in Lane County with modern pipe materials. The one key separation rule: where a water line crosses above a sewer line, the bottom of the water pipe must be at least 12 inches above the top of the sewer pipe (OPSC 720.1). Individual utility companies may have additional separation specifications beyond code minimums — confirm these when you initiate the service application.
