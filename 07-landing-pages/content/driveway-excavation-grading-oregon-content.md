## What's the difference between driveway grading and driveway excavation?

These two terms get used interchangeably, but they describe different scopes of work, and knowing which one you need keeps you from paying for more than you have to.

Excavation is the initial cut. Existing soil is removed down to firm ground — typically 8 to 12 inches for a new Lane County driveway — and the foundation bed is established. You need excavation when you're building a new driveway from scratch or when the existing base has failed and clay pumping has turned it to mud.

Grading is the shaping step. Existing material is redistributed to re-establish the proper slope, crown, and drainage angles so water runs off the surface instead of through it. A driveway with a sound base that's just spread out or compacted into flat ruts is usually a regrading job, not an excavation job.

New driveways need both. Before D&D quotes either scope, we assess the base condition first. If it's sound, you get a regrading estimate. If it's failed, we tell you that before you spend money on a surface fix that won't hold.

## Why do gravel driveways fail in Oregon?

Oregon driveways fail because of Lane County's clay soil and seven months of rain — not because gravel driveways don't work. The failure mode tells you what the actual fix is.

1. Gravel migration and washout. The surface is flat or crowned the wrong direction. Water channels straight down the driving surface and carries rock to the low end. No ditch alongside the drive means water that should drain away stays on the road instead. This is the most common failure on rural Lane County driveways.

2. Rutting. Two causes: base too thin, or clay punching through with no geotextile separator. Tire tracks become depressions. Depressions fill with water. Water softens the clay beneath. The ruts deepen. This is Malpass clay doing what Hydrologic Group D soil does when the base course doesn't separate it from the gravel above.

3. Washboard ripples. Surface instability under repeated traffic on a soft, undifferentiated layer. Usually a symptom of a thin or missing base rather than a surface-only problem.

4. Soft spots and mud pumping. Saturated clay pumps upward through base rock, mixing fines into the gravel and creating soft spots that never fully firm up. This is the classic sign of a driveway built without geotextile fabric on Lane County clay. Once it starts, it doesn't stop on its own.

5. Edge erosion. No shoulder grading; gravel slides off both sides under traffic. Common on driveways cut into hillsides.

Adding gravel to a driveway with bad drainage solves nothing permanently — the rock leaves with the next rain. The fix starts with a drainage diagnosis: crown, ditching, and culvert condition, before a single yard of new material goes in.

## How deep should a gravel driveway base be in Lane County?

No published ODOT or Lane County code specifies a residential gravel driveway depth — that standard governs public road construction, not private driveways. What exists is industry consensus, and it converges consistently across Oregon contractors who work on Willamette Valley clay:

| Layer | Material | Depth |
|---|---|---|
| Subgrade prep | Non-woven geotextile fabric over native clay | — |
| Base course | 2-inch minus crushed rock, compacted in 4–6 inch lifts | 6–12 inches |
| Wearing course | 3/4-minus crushed quarry rock | 2–4 inches |

For passenger-vehicle residential use on stable subgrade, 6 inches is the accepted floor. On Lane County's Malpass clay — which is what most of the Eugene metro, Junction City, Creswell, and the surrounding valley floor sits on — 8 to 12 inches is the working standard. Farm driveways or anything that sees heavy delivery or equipment traffic warrants 10 to 12 inches or more.

The geotextile fabric is not optional on Lane County clay. Malpass clay is classified as Hydrologic Soil Group D: very slow water transmission, high shrink-swell potential, seasonally high water table. Without a woven fabric separator between the clay subgrade and the base rock, the clay and gravel mix within one to two wet seasons. Once that happens, the base loses load-bearing strength and no amount of surface gravel fixes it. Contractors who skip the fabric to save $200–$400 on the install cost the homeowner the driveway in two winters.

Base rock goes in at 4-to-6-inch lifts, each layer compacted before the next goes in. On saturated clay, which describes most of Lane County from October through April, rushing compaction produces a surface that looks right until the first hard rain.

## What type of gravel is best for Oregon driveways?

The standard surface course for Willamette Valley driveways is 3/4-minus crushed quarry rock: angular material that compacts under traffic and stays put. This is the same material the State of Oregon uses on gravel roads, and it's what D&D uses on Lane County driveways. In Oregon the product is called "3/4 minus" — what you might know from other regions as "road base" or "crusher run" at the surface layer is a different thing here.

The base course runs below it: 2-to-4-inch minus crushed rock, also sold as road base or CSBC (Crushed Surface Base Course). Angular, compactable, structural.

Pea gravel and rounded river rock don't compact. They roll out of place under traffic and wash out in rain. Pit run, common east of the Cascades, has inconsistent gradation and performs poorly on clay subgrades in the wet Willamette Valley climate.

D&D sources material from regional quarries that supply the Lane County market. The vocabulary and gradations are specific to Oregon's supply chain; what works in Central Oregon or the Portland metro isn't always the same product available here.

## When is the best time of year to schedule driveway work in Lane County?

May through September is the best window for major excavation and base work in Lane County. During the dry season, Malpass clay firms up enough to compact properly, equipment can mobilize without bogging, and material can be placed and graded in a single pass.

Spring (March to May) is the right time to address winter damage while the ground is still workable. You can see what the wet season did to the driveway, the clay hasn't dried to concrete, and you have months before the next rain cycle.

Fall (August to October) is the best time to get ahead of the problem: regrade the surface before heavy rain season so the driveway enters winter with proper crown and drainage, not flat spots that will hold water for the next seven months.

Major excavation during the wet season (October through April) is possible but risks compaction quality on saturated clay. Emergency regrading after storm damage can be done year-round; a follow-up pass once the ground dries may be needed. Lane County sits in Zone 8a/8b — frost heave isn't the primary threat to driveways here. Wet-season saturation is.

## When can I regrade my driveway myself, and when should I call a contractor?

DIY makes sense for small, isolated ruts under an inch deep in a confined section: rake existing material over the low spot, add matching gravel, compact by hand or with a rented plate compactor.

Beyond that the returns diminish quickly. Re-establishing crown across a full driveway length, culvert work, washboard correction, and base repair all require a motor grader or a tractor with a box blade. Any problem that has come back more than twice after you've regraded it yourself is a contractor job — the issue is below the surface, not on it.

If you're putting gravel on the same section for the third time, a professional assessment costs nothing. D&D estimates are free and include a drainage review, not just a material quote.
